# The experience

*What we are trying to make someone feel and notice.*

This is the short account of why komorebi exists. Everything in the model serves it, and when a later decision is ambiguous, this is the arbiter. The point was never to reproduce a reference image; it is to recreate something that was *felt*.

## The afternoon in the park

This began on an afternoon under the trees in a park, in **early spring**, the leaves not yet fully grown and the canopy still sparse.

On the ground were the familiar dapples of light. Looked at closely, they were not crisp. They were **soft, out of focus**, like looking through an old prime lens, the kind with character. And the focus was not fixed: thick and thin clouds drifted across the sun, and as they passed the whole pattern **breathed in and out of focus**, blooming soft and then sharpening again. The bokeh on the ground was the thing: that very out-of-focus, glowing, low-contrast quality.

Then the wind, and two motions remembered vividly.

**A strong directional breeze** pushed all the leaves to one side, so the gaps on the ground **translated almost perfectly**, sliding sideways while keeping their shapes. You could feel the breathing of the wind, an easing-in rather than a switch flipping. Because branches can only move so far, at the peak of a gust you could feel the **elasticity of the branches fighting the wind**, and when the wind died they returned to where they had started. And there was a **backlash** at that stiffening: bent hard against their limit, the branches recoiled, snapping back past rest and settling rather than gliding home.

**A very faint wind, in all directions at once**, the kind that seems to come up when the sun shines a little stronger. This did not translate anything. It made the leaves **rearrange**, opening entirely new gaps. Because the foliage was sparse, individual leaves still mattered: moving one genuinely changed a gap, so the pattern reorganised into something wholly new. It looked, unmistakably, like **water caustics moving across the floor of a pool as waves collapse**: bright shapes merging, splitting and dissolving rather than sliding.

Under the very faintest breeze the shapes seemed to **glisten**, a fine twinkling in place.

## The calm beneath it

Beneath the optics, the afternoon carried a feeling the piece must carry too: **calm**. The mindfulness of lying under the trees; peace, an innate selfness, the oneness with nature. Anyone in front of this should feel that.

It is an arbiter beside the physics. The piece never startles, never performs, never asks for attention. It is a place a person settles into, not a thing they operate. Where a decision passes every technical test but disturbs the stillness, the stillness wins.

Calm can hold contrast. A stronger gust followed by a long settling, or a brief opening that admits an unusually clear sun-image, can deepen the repose around it, so long as the event belongs to the scene and follows from the same causes as the quiet. What calm forbids is performance: a cadence of effects, a programmed sweep, anything that makes the viewer wait for the next thing.

## The other places

The afternoon widened into other rooms, each a north star of its own.

**The bedroom curtain.** Sunset through a backyard tree onto a moss-green velvet drape, seen from the bed. The dapples do not lie on a floor; they stand up a vertical cloth, lit from behind. Near the glass the window's bars throw their shape sharp; the tree, metres away, throws soft images of the sun. Both in one frame, and the cloth glowing dimly from within. **Sought, not yet reached:** the curtain is built, but its scene has not yet made someone feel that evening.

**The tent.** Komorebi on the nylon of a tent pitched under trees at midday, seen from inside, lying down. Canopy overhead, light falling onto the panels around you, and the detail that carries the whole read: *different panels at different brightnesses, because they face different ways*. Some patches hard bright spots, others diffuse glow. A small room you are inside of, not a corridor.

**The hammock.** Lying back at a festival, tall trunks converging overhead, crowns lit from behind against the bright sky, the whole grove leaning together with the gusts. It is the founding afternoon with the eyes turned the other way: the same grove and the same sun, no longer read off the floor but looked at. Here the tree is the subject; it has to read as dark, connected wood, and the leaves as lanterns against the light, with the sun leaking through the gaps.

**The backyard at night.** The piece thrown onto a yard with a real projector. A projector is a hard rectangle; a canopy has no frame. The light should pool in the grass and fade, not show its edge.

## Autumn

The trees were watched through a fall, and the season is now part of the piece.

What matters most about the colour of the trees is **how one tree can be red while the one next to it is yellow or green**; why that happens, how they are organised in nature, how the information is scattered or grouped. Within one tree the sunlit crown turns first and reddest and the shaded interior stays yellower longer. Trees and buildings shade each other, and which goes red first follows that geography.

A maple leaf held up to the sun looks like a **maze**: cells with white walls between them, like lizard skin, and the colour darkening into the red. Thirteen maples were photographed; they do not all look alike, and the engine's leaves should not either.

One all-yellow tree had half its leaves on the ground within ten days, the sunlit ones first. The beech keeps about half of its leaves, brown, on the tree through the season. **One day after rain many leaves fell**, and the carpet lay darker and more saturated for being wet. A fallen leaf is a different object from a hanging one: it casts nothing, it is seen by reflection not transmission, and it ages red to copper to brown.

In autumn the dusk law inverts: the sun-images stay white-gold and it is the **shade** that goes warm, because the colour lives in what the leaves pass, not in the light that reaches them.

## Where it was lived

Many of these afternoons happened in and around Montréal, Montebello, Plaisance and Ottawa: a park on the mountain, a backyard, a tent under maples, a fall by the river. The piece is deeply grateful to Canadian nature, which gave it the light it tries to carry.

## What all of it asks for

A place. The larger-scale composition of a scene, what surrounds the viewer and what is implied beyond the frame, matters as much as the detail inside any leaf. Looking away and back should find somewhere recognisable, its light and motion still moving. The question every piece of work is finally judged by: **does this become somewhere I recognise and want to remain?**
